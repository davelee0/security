# Economics, Insurance and Funding: Cybersecurity for Small Organizations Holding Children's Data

Lens: security economist and insurance analyst. Research date: 2026-11-23. Method: web search results only (no full-page fetches), so most figures come from search snippets and secondary write-ups. Treat numbers as "reported by" rather than verified against primary PDFs unless noted. Hypothesis tested: the binding constraint is attention and incentives, not tools or money, and insurers, funders and procurement are stronger levers than guides.

## 1. Cyber insurance for schools, nonprofits and religious organizations (2024-2026)

### Takeaway
Insurers have become the de facto security regulator for organizations that buy cyber cover. MFA, EDR and isolated/offline backups are the standard underwriting gate, and the claims mix (email fraud at about 60% of small-business claims) points straight at MFA and payment-verification controls. The three big US religious insurers (Catholic Mutual, Church Mutual, Brotherhood Mutual) all offer cyber cover and free educational material, but I found no public evidence that they enforce specific technical controls for small parishes and schools. That is a lever largely unused.

### Cited Findings
**Market and required controls (K-12 / education)**
- Broker outlook (Q2 2026): education is in "sustained hardening", but capacity is improving and prices are moderating. The PowerSchool incident tightened core control standards. — [IMA Financial Group, Education Markets In Focus Q2 2026](https://imacorp.com/insights/education-markets-in-focus-q2-2026)
- Baseline controls insurers ask K-12 to prove: MFA, endpoint detection and response (EDR), isolated or offline backups, an incident response plan and phishing training. — [Cybersecure California K-12 checklist](https://cybersecureca.com/k12/cyber-insurance-readiness-k12/); [Marsh McLennan Agency](https://www.marshmma.com/us/insights/details/cyber-security-in-schools.html)
- Districts without MFA for remote access face denials or surcharges (vendor claim). — [ResoluteGuard](https://resoluteguard.com/what-cyber-insurers-now-require-k12-districts-to-prove/)
- Vendor claim of K-12 premium increases of "up to 300%" for 2024-2025. This is unverified marketing and should not be cited as fact. — [Five Star Technology Solutions](https://fivestartech.com/navigating-the-changing-cyber-insurance-landscape-for-k-12-schools/)
- 87% of independent (private) K-12 schools buy a $1M cyber limit, and 13% buy $2M or more (2025 benchmark). — [Unison Risk Advisors 2025 Education Benchmark Reports](https://unisonriskadvisors.com/news/2025-education-benchmark-reports)

**Claims patterns**
- Coalition 2025 Cyber Claims Report (2024 claims): BEC plus funds transfer fraud (FTF) made up about 60% of claims, and ransomware about 21%. BEC severity rose 23% to an average loss of $35,000. 29% of BEC cases led to a fraudulent transfer. Coalition clawed back $31M in stolen funds. — [Coalition 2025 Cyber Claims Report](https://www.coalitioninc.com/blog/2025-cyber-claims-report); [SC World](https://www.scworld.com/news/most-cyber-insurance-claims-stem-from-bec-fraud-report-says)
- Coalition 2026 report (2025 claims): BEC plus FTF made up 58% of claims. FTF frequency fell 18%. BEC claims rose about 30% versus 2H 2024, which Coalition attributes to AI phishing kits aimed at smaller businesses. Firms with more than $100M revenue had about 5 times the claim frequency of smaller firms. — [Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/business-email-compromise-claims-jump-30-as-a-i-reshapes-cyber-risk/); [Help Net Security, Mar 2026](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/03/06/cyber-claims-report-ransomware-gangs-data-theft/)
- Help Net Security on the 2026 report: backups are working, and ransomware gangs are responding with data theft. That matters for children's-data holders, because the extortion lever shifts from availability to confidentiality. — [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/03/06/cyber-claims-report-ransomware-gangs-data-theft/)
- NetDiligence 2025 Cyber Claims Study: 10,402 claims (2020-2024). I did not find an education-specific breakdown. — via [ResoluteGuard](https://resoluteguard.com/what-cyber-insurers-now-require-k12-districts-to-prove/)
- Sophos State of Ransomware in Education 2025 (441 victims surveyed): lower-education recovery cost, excluding ransom, fell 39% to about $2.28M, still the highest of any sector. The Sophos blog says $2.20M. Lower-education ransom payments were about $800K. Sophos calls this an average; Infosecurity calls it a median, which is a conflict. About half of lower-ed victims paid. 50% recovered within a week, up from 30%. — [Sophos press release, Sep 2025](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/press-releases/2025/09/sophos-ransomware-education-report-2025); [Sophos blog](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/the-state-of-ransomware-in-education-2025); [Infosecurity Magazine](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/ransomware-payments-plummet/)

**Religious-organization insurers**
- Catholic Mutual Group: a nonprofit founded in 1889 that insures only Church-owned institutions and serves more than 125 dioceses and archdioceses. Its program includes a cyber liability line ("covers hardware, software and operations from the effects of hacking, malware, etc."). — [Archdiocese of Milwaukee CMG summary, Nov 2024](https://www.archmil.org/ArchMil/Resources/ParFin/202411CatholicMutualGroup.pdf); [Dec 2025 version](https://intranet.archmil.org/ArchMil/Resources/ParFin/202512CatholicMutualGroup.pdf)
- A 2022 Gallagher deck for diocesan clients said insurers began requiring all sites to meet stricter requirements starting in FY22. It listed security awareness training as a condition of cyber cover and said "MFA for everyone will most likely be a requirement." It does not name CMG, and it is dated. — [Gallagher Convocation 2022](https://www.ajg.com/us/-/media/files/gallagher/us/events/convocation-2022-workshops/coming-together-to-achieve-the-right-cyber-coverage-for-the-diocesan-enterprise.pdf)
- Church Mutual: sells cyber cover through partner Beazley, which includes breach response and data recovery. It publishes free resources: "10 Essentials of Cybersecurity", a cybersecurity assessment, and MFA and password guidance. — [Church Mutual cybersecurity](https://churchmutual.com/cybersecurity); [Church Mutual / Beazley partner page](https://www.churchmutual.com/resource-center/partner-services/beazley); [Church Mutual cyber liability](https://www.churchmutual.com/insurance/cyber-liability-insurance)
- Brotherhood Mutual: has its own cyber liability product. Limits run from $50K to $6M, with a detailed questionnaire above $1M; that figure is from about 2019. It offers a free Safety Library with cyber checklists, ransomware guidance and an on-demand webinar. — [Brotherhood Mutual cyber liability](https://www.brotherhoodmutual.com/insurance/cyber-liability-insurance/); [Cybersecurity: Available Resources](https://www.brotherhoodmutual.com/resources/safety-library/risk-management-articles/administrative-staff-and-finance/documents-and-data/cyber-security-available-resources/); [Cyber Security Checklist](https://brotherhoodmutual.com/safety-library/cyber-security-checklist)
- Unrelated but relevant to the insurer relationship: in Nov 2026, Catholic Mutual Relief Society sued in federal court, saying it has "no coverage obligations" in Louisiana abuse suits. This signals strained diocese-insurer relations and capital pressure from abuse liabilities. — [EWTN News](https://www.ewtnnews.com/world/us/in-federal-lawsuit-catholic-aid-company-says-it-has-no-obligations-in-louisiana-sex-abuse-suits); [Catholic World Report](https://www.catholicworldreport.com/2026/11/19/in-federal-lawsuit-catholic-aid-company-says-it-has-no-obligations-in-louisiana-sex-abuse-suits/)

### Inferences
- Email fraud (BEC/FTF) is about 60% of small-business claims. For parishes, schools and youth nonprofits that handle tuition, payroll and vendor payments, two cheap controls carry most of the expected-loss reduction: phishing-resistant MFA on email, and call-back verification of payment changes. A volunteer program should lead with these, not with EDR.
- Diocesan programs pool risk centrally (CMG insures the diocese, which covers its parishes and schools). The diocese, therefore, is the natural "procurement and underwriting" chokepoint. A diocese can require controls of its parishes and schools as a condition of being covered under the master policy. This is a stronger lever than guides sent to individual principals.
- Religious insurers currently compete on education content, not enforced controls. If one of them added a premium credit or required MFA attestation, it could shift behavior across thousands of small entities at near-zero cost to the insurer. This is the central test of the "insurers as lever" hypothesis, and an advocacy target.
- Small orgs with low limits ($1M) and no dedicated IT often buy cover, if at all, as a bundled endorsement with light underwriting. In that setting the insurer's control-enforcement lever is weak, which is consistent with Woods and Moore (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No primary CMG cyber application or underwriting questionnaire was found. I could not confirm whether CMG requires MFA, EDR or backups, what limits it offers, or whether it provides free services such as scanning or IR hotlines.
- No 2024-2026 premium figures specific to small private or parochial schools, childcare centers or youth nonprofits.
- No evidence found of coverage withdrawals or exclusions specifically targeting schools in 2024-2026, beyond general hardening commentary.
- NetDiligence and Coalition education/nonprofit sub-segment claim data was not found in the snippets.

## 2. Federal funding and free government services: E-Rate Cyber Pilot, SLCGP, CISA, MS-ISAC

### Takeaway
Federal money for this population is shrinking or narrowly targeted. The $200M FCC pilot is closed to new entrants and only partly committed. SLCGP lapsed in Sept 2025, got a stopgap and has an uncertain reauthorization, and it flows to state and local government, not private schools. MS-ISAC moved to paid membership on Oct 1, 2025 and lost many members. CISA's free scanning remains available and underused. The money exists in pockets; the binding constraint is that small orgs don't know about it or can't do the paperwork.

### Cited Findings
**FCC Schools and Libraries Cybersecurity Pilot Program**
- Three-year program, up to $200M from the Universal Service Fund, administered by USAC. It also serves as a test of whether USF should fund cybersecurity long-term. — [USAC Cybersecurity Pilot Program](https://www.usac.org/e-rate/cybersecurity-pilot-program/); [FCC program page](https://www.fcc.gov/cybersecurity-pilot-program)
- Jan 16, 2025 Public Notice (DA-25-53) selected 707 participants: about 645 schools and districts, 50 libraries and 12 consortia. They were chosen from more than 2,700 applicants requesting $3.7B, which means the pilot was oversubscribed about 18 to 1 by dollars. — [FCC Public Notice DA-25-53A1](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-53A1.pdf); [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2025/01/17/fcc-cybersecurity-pilot-participants-selected.aspx); [GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/fcc-announces-school-library-cyber-pilot-participants)
- Eligibility: the pilot uses E-Rate definitions of school and library, so any E-Rate-eligible entity was eligible. E-Rate eligibility generally covers private and parochial non-profit schools, though I did not verify this against a source here. — [FCC Cybersecurity Pilot FAQs](https://www.fcc.gov/cybersecurity-pilot/frequently-asked-questions)
- The FCC 471 window closed Sep 15, 2025. The first funding commitment decision letters (FCDLs) came Dec 2025: $18.8M to 140 applicants. A USAC newsletter (Mar 2026) reports $49.8M committed after the second wave on Feb 25, 2026. A state guide reports $129.2M committed to 571 participants across eight waves (Dec 17, 2025 to Jul 22, 2026), and says no new participants are being added. — [Funds For Learning](https://www.fundsforlearning.com/news/fcc-releases-cybersecurity-pilot-funding-request-data-early-insights/); [USAC March 2026 newsletter](https://www.usac.org/wp-content/uploads/e-rate/documents/cyber_pilot/newsletters/March-2026-Newsletter.pdf); [LTC Illinois](https://www.ltcillinois.org/resources/cybersecurity-pilot-program/); [Funds For Learning, second wave](https://www.fundsforlearning.com/news/second-wave-of-e-rate-cyber-pilot-decisions-larger-awards-clearer-patterns/)
- The FCC declined to add advanced cybersecurity to the FY2026 E-Rate Eligible Services List, so the pilot remains the only E-Rate-adjacent path. — [ArmorStack summary](https://armorstack.ai/e-rate/cybersecurity-pilot/); [Cisco blog FY2026](https://blogs.cisco.com/industries/navigating-e-rate-for-fy2026-key-deadlines-and-new-opportunities)

**State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program (SLCGP)**
- Created under the IIJA. States must pass through at least 80% to local governments. — [FEMA fact sheet](https://www.fema.gov/print/txt/node/703003); [WaTech](https://watech.wa.gov/state-local-cybersecurity-grant-program)
- Lapsed Sep 30, 2025. A short extension rode on the fall 2025 funding stopgap. — [Nextgov, Nov 2025](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2025/11/government-funding-deal-reups-cyber-grant-program/409498/)
- PILLAR Act (H.R. 5078): passed the House by voice vote in Nov 2025, for a roughly 10-year reauthorization. End dates conflict: FY2033 vs FY2035 vs "seven years". It specifies no dollar amount, and Senate status was pending as of late-2025 reporting. — [House Homeland Security Committee](https://homeland.house.gov/2025/11/18/pillar-act-garners-broad-industry-and-government-support-passes-house/); [Congress.gov H.R.5078](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5078/all-info); [StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/slcgp-house-pillar-act-passes/); [Governing](https://www.governing.com/policy/states-and-localities-may-get-more-federal-cybersecurity-money)
- 2026 status: states are still running rounds on earlier awards. Wisconsin's deadline was Oct 30, 2026, and Maryland announced opportunities in Aug 2026. Oregon's SLCGP opportunity was "temporarily closed" as of Apr 1, 2026. Texas lists an FY2026 and FY2027 mitigation round. NCSL (June 2026) asked for $300M for FY2027. — [Wisconsin DET](https://det.wi.gov/Pages/Cybersecurity_Grants.aspx); [Maryland](https://news.maryland.gov/mdem/2026/08/24/maryland-announces-cybersecurity-funding-opportunities-for-state-and-local-governments/); [Oregon OEM](https://www.oregon.gov/oem/emresources/grants/pages/state-and-local-cybersecurity-grant-program.aspx); [Texas eGrants FY2027](https://egrants.gov.texas.gov/fundingopp/state-and-local-cybersecurity-grant-program-slcgp-mitigation-projects-fy2027); [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/news/details/ncsl-urges-funding-for-state-and-local-cybersecurity-grant-program)

**CISA free services**
- Cyber Hygiene (vulnerability scanning) is free. CISA's K-12 toolkit and the FCC's resource list both recommend that schools enroll. About 1,500 schools had signed up, per an older K-12 Dive interview (out of roughly 130,000 US K-12 schools, an inference). Enrollment is by email to vulnerability@cisa.dhs.gov with the subject "Requesting Cyber Hygiene Services". — [CISA Cyber Hygiene Services](https://www.cisa.gov/cyber-hygiene-services); [CISA K-12 toolkit](https://www.cisa.gov/online-toolkit-partnering-safeguard-k-12-organizations-cybersecurity-threats); [FCC Cybersecurity Resource Recommendations](https://fcc.gov/sites/default/files/Cybersecurity-Resource-Recommendations.pdf); [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/cisa-k12-cybersecurity-resources-frazier/720436/)
- CISA's Jan 2023 K-12 report remains the core federal guidance. — [CISA K-12 report](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/K-12report-24Jan23.pdf)
- CISA also publishes resources specifically for cybersecurity clinics. — [CISA Resources for Cybersecurity Clinics (Feb 2024)](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-02/Resources-for-Cybersecurity-Clinics-508c%20%281%29%20%281%29.pdf)

**MS-ISAC**
- CISA cut $10M in March 2025, about half the budget. The remaining cooperative agreement expired Sept 2025. MS-ISAC went fee-based with no federal funding on Oct 1, 2025. CISA said it would "fill the gap" with some services. — [CIS MS-ISAC Membership FAQ](https://www.cisecurity.org/ms-isac/ms-isac-membership-faq); [NACo](https://www.naco.org/news/cybersecurity-group-retools-after-funding-cut); [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/09/30/cisa-ms-isac-funding/); [Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/us-cuts-funding-ms-isac/)
- Dues for single organizations with budgets under $25M top out at $1,495 a year, with a possible first-year hardship waiver. Membership "plummeted". CIS still subsidizes some of the smallest localities. — [Cybersecurity Dive](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/ms-isac-membership-loss-states-federal-funding-cut/821984/)
- Lost free services that K-12 relied on included malicious domain blocking (MDBR), plain-language alerts and network monitoring. — [Armorpoint](https://armorpoint.com/2025/10/24/what-the-ms-isac-transition-means-for-k-12-and-public-education/); [Scamdrill](https://scamdrill.com/blog/k12-ransomware-after-federal-cuts)

### Inferences
- Private, Catholic and classical schools, childcare centers and youth nonprofits are mostly outside SLCGP (a government pass-through) and outside MS-ISAC's core public-sector constituency. Their realistic federal touchpoints are E-Rate, if they already file, and CISA Cyber Hygiene. Many small private schools don't file E-Rate at all because of the administrative burden. That shows "free money" is gated by attention and paperwork capacity, which supports the hypothesis.
- The pilot was 18x oversubscribed and only around 65% committed about 18 months later. That suggests demand from organized districts with E-Rate consultants, and an administrative bottleneck, rather than a lack of funds. Small private schools likely lose to large districts in this kind of process.
- A volunteer project can add value by doing the enrollment work itself: CISA Cyber Hygiene sign-ups and Quad9 or other free protective DNS as an MDBR replacement. Each is a 15-minute task that small orgs don't get around to.

### Gaps
- No count of private or parochial or Catholic schools among the 707 pilot participants. This would require parsing the DA-25-53 participant list.
- No confirmed status on whether the PILLAR Act or a Senate equivalent became law, or FY2026 SLCGP appropriations, as of Nov 2026.
- CISA Cyber Hygiene eligibility for private schools and 501(c)(3)s was not confirmed in snippets. Historically CISA has served critical-infrastructure private entities, but this needs confirmation from the CISA page.
- Effects of 2025-2026 CISA staffing and budget cuts on Cyber Hygiene capacity were not established.

## 3. Free and discounted tech programs and their uptake

### Takeaway
Free security-grade tooling still exists (Microsoft Business Basic for nonprofits, Google Workspace for Education and Nonprofits, Cloudflare Project Galileo, CRI and GCA toolkits). But the most security-relevant Microsoft grant ended in July 2025, and uptake data is almost nonexistent. Galileo's roughly 3,000-3,400 protected properties is tiny relative to the 1.5M+ US nonprofits.

### Cited Findings
- Microsoft ended the free Business Premium (up to 10 seats) and Office 365 E1 grants effective July 1, 2025, as of each organization's first renewal after that date. They were replaced by a free Business Basic for up to 300 users and about a 75% discount on Business Premium, roughly $5.50 per user per month. The transition is not automatic. — [Withum](https://www.withum.com/resources/microsoft-nonprofit-pricing-changes-grant-donation-365-business-premium-e1/); [BTI Group](https://www.btigroup.com/post/microsoft-365-nonprofits-churches/); [ABM](https://abmnow.com/2025/05/15/microsoft-is-ending-its-business-premium-and-e1-grants-for-nonprofits/); [Plain English Technology](https://plainenglishtechnology.com/microsoft-365s-changes-to-nonprofit-licensing/)
- Cloudflare Project Galileo protected 3,000+ internet properties (Dec 2025 impact report) and 3,400+ websites in 120 countries (12-year post, 2026). It relies on 56 civil-society partners to vet applicants, and blocked 108.9B threats between May 2024 and Mar 2025. — [Cloudflare impact report press release, Dec 19 2025](https://www.cloudflare.com/press/press-releases/2025/cloudflare-publishes-fifth-annual-impact-report/); [Celebrating 12 years of Project Galileo](https://blog.cloudflare.com/celebrating-12-years-of-project-galileo/); [Celebrating 11 years](https://blog.cloudflare.com/celebrating-11-years-of-project-galileo-global-impact)

### Inferences
- The Microsoft change moved Business Premium from free to paid. Business Premium is where Defender for Business, Intune and Conditional Access live, so it is the security bundle. For a 15-person parish school, this is about $1,000 a year, small in dollars but large as a new budget line in a cash-constrained org. Expect many small nonprofits to have quietly downgraded to Business Basic and lost Conditional Access and endpoint protection without realizing. This is a concrete, checkable risk for a volunteer audit.
- Security defaults (MFA) are available in the free Business Basic and in Google Workspace tiers. Lack of MFA in small orgs therefore can't be blamed on cost, which supports the attention hypothesis.
- Galileo's referral-partner model is an example of a funder or intermediary lever: uptake runs through trusted intermediaries, not self-service. A diocese or a childcare association could play that role.

### Gaps
- No data found on Google for Nonprofits or Google Workspace for Education security-feature adoption (for example, the percentage enforcing 2SV).
- No search was completed on Cloudflare Project Athenian, Cyber Readiness Institute (Cyber Readiness Program) or Global Cyber Alliance toolkit uptake. I did not have enough tool budget, so this needs follow-up.
- No utilization rates for any free program among schools or childcare specifically.

## 4. Market failures: who bears the loss from a children's data breach

### Takeaway
This is a textbook externality. The institution controls security spending, but much of the harm (child identity fraud discovered years later) falls on families, and the liability system rarely routes the cost back to small institutions. Class actions target deep-pocket vendors (PowerSchool), not small schools. So small organizations face weak private incentives, and the real exposure is concentrated in third-party edtech vendors.

### Cited Findings
- Javelin 2022 Child Identity Fraud Study: 915,000 US children were identity-fraud victims in the past year, at an average of $1,128 per affected household. 1.7M (1 in 43) had data exposed in breaches. An earlier Javelin study estimated more than $540M in out-of-pocket family costs (2017). — [Javelin via PRWeb](https://www.prweb.com/releases/1-7-million-u-s-children-fell-victim-to-data-breaches-according-to-javelin-s-2022-child-identity-fraud-study-851086633.html); [DataBreaches.net on 2018 study](https://databreaches.net/2018/04/25/1-million-us-children-affected-by-identity-theft-last-year-infuriating-study-finds/)
- Javelin: 1 in 8 US children has had identity data compromised in a breach in the past six years (cited Jan 2025). — [PaymentsJournal](https://www.paymentsjournal.com/stealing-childrens-identities-the-threat-that-parents-overlook/amp/)
- Child identity theft often goes undetected until the child applies for credit or student loans, sometimes more than a decade later. — [PaymentsJournal](https://www.paymentsjournal.com/stealing-childrens-identities-the-threat-that-parents-overlook/amp/); [PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2580629/powerschool-hackers-have-your-kids-info-these-3-steps-will-protect-them.html)
- PowerSchool breach (Dec 2024): about 62.5M students and 9.5M teachers across more than 6,500 districts, with SSNs exposed for many young children. An internal report said the firm missed a basic security step (MFA on a support credential). More than 30 federal class actions were filed by families, and a Wisconsin district sued for breach of contract. Families were offered credit monitoring. — [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/news/PowerSchool-data-breach-Explaining-how-it-happened/872445/); [Yahoo/AP-style report on internal review](https://www.yahoo.com/news/childrens-data-hacked-school-software-120000485.html); [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/wisconsin-district-sues-ed-tech-giant-powerschool-after-massive-data-breach/); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/01/10/powerschool-hack-identity-theft-data-breach)

### Inferences
- Loss allocation:
  - Families bear identity-fraud costs, which are delayed, hard to attribute and therefore hard to sue over.
  - Institutions bear operational disruption and recovery. Sophos puts this at about $2.2M average for lower-ed ransomware victims, but small private schools are poorly represented in that average.
  - Insurers bear covered first-party costs.
  - Vendors bear class-action exposure.
- Because harm is delayed and diffuse, a small school's expected liability is low, and its rational security spend is below the social optimum (Anderson and Moore's misaligned-incentives argument). This argues for levers that do not depend on liability: insurer conditions, diocesan or association mandates, and funder or grant conditions.
- PowerSchool shows that vendor and procurement risk dominates for small organizations. A parish school's children's data mostly lives in SaaS (SIS, tuition management, childcare apps). Procurement terms, such as requiring vendor MFA, breach notification and data minimization, are a high-leverage, low-cost intervention that a diocese or association can impose once for many schools.
- Low-cost family-side mitigations (child credit freezes) shift protection to the bearer of harm. A school could include this in its breach-response template.

### Gaps
- No small-school or childcare-specific breach cost data was found. Survey reports (Sophos, IBM) skew toward larger institutions.
- State law on child-data breach liability (for example, New York SHIELD Act or Ed Law 2-d applicability to private schools) was not researched here.
- I found no recent (2024-2026) Javelin child-fraud cost update.

## 5. Evidence on which interventions change small-organization behavior

### Takeaway
Rigorous causal evidence is thin. The best qualitative work suggests insurance does prompt baseline controls (MFA, patching), but often only to the minimum required, with weak premium incentives and a "false sense of security." This partly supports the hypothesis: insurers are a real lever, but only when they enforce checkable conditions.

### Cited Findings
- RUSI Occasional Paper (Nurse, Sullivan, MacColl, 2021): insurers' ability to improve policyholder security varies with insurer maturity. The authors recommend insurers agree on common minimum security requirements for SMEs (11-250 employees). — [RUSI OP: Cyber Insurance and the Cyber Security Challenge](https://static.rusi.org/247-op-cyber-insurance-v2.pdf); [Kent repository copy](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/89041/1/RUSI-Kent-OP-Cyber-insurance.pdf)
- RUSI "Cyber Insurance and the Ransomware Challenge" covers insurer influence on ransomware resilience. — [RUSI OP](https://static.rusi.org/OP-cyber-insurance-ransomware-challenge-web-final.pdf)
- Woods and Moore, "Does Insurance Have a Future in Governing Cybersecurity?" (IEEE S&P, 2019): interviews suggest financial incentives are weak; standards accreditation brings no "massive discount." — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335764473_Does_Insurance_Have_a_Future_in_Governing_Cybersecurity)
- Woods et al., underwriting and claims data study: insurers collect limited security data at underwriting. — [arXiv 2008.04713](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.04713)
- 2025 SME study (Computers & Security): insurance-prompted improvements mirror Cyber Essentials (MFA, password hygiene, patching, firewall config). But participants admitted insurance gave a false sense of security, and they met only minimum requirements rather than improving continuously. — [ScienceDirect, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167404825005073)
- Coalition attributes falling claim severity in part to improved defensive posture among insureds. Coalition is an active insurer that scans policyholders. — [Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/cyber-claims-frequency-rises-but-severity-falls-as-businesses-improve-defensive-posture/)
- Sophos: education recovery speed improved (50% within a week, up from 30%), consistent with better backups, a control insurers push. — [Sophos press release](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/press-releases/2025/09/sophos-ransomware-education-report-2025)

### Inferences
- Insurer requirements work for discrete, attestable controls (MFA, backups). They don't produce a continuous security culture. For a volunteer project, that suggests piggybacking on renewal deadlines, the moment of maximum attention. An example: "your renewal questionnaire asks about MFA; we'll help you make it true."
- Mandates from a central body (a diocese, a childcare licensing authority, a funder grant condition) likely work through the same mechanism as insurers: a checkable condition tied to money or permission to operate.
- Pure information interventions (guides, checklists) appear in every insurer's free library, yet the claims mix is still dominated by basic email fraud. That is indirect support that information alone is not the binding constraint.

### Gaps
- No randomized or quasi-experimental study was found on interventions for schools or nonprofits specifically.
- No study was found on peer pressure or association mandates (for example, NCEA or state Catholic conferences) as security levers.
- I found no outcome measurement (before/after security posture) from clinic engagements; see section 6.

## 6. Cybersecurity clinics model

### Takeaway
University cybersecurity clinics are scaling fast on philanthropic money, mostly Google.org and Craig Newmark Philanthropies. The national consortium reports about 700 organizations served per year. NYC now has a clinic: NYU's, launched in summer 2026, which explicitly names schools and nonprofits and works with NYC Cyber Command. That makes it a natural partner. No Catholic-school-specific clinic was found.

### Inferences
- Clinic economics: roughly $0.5-1M of philanthropic seed per clinic, against about 700 organizations a year across 61 clinics. That is about 11 organizations per clinic per year, so capacity is the constraint, not demand. This points to a "force multiplier" role for a volunteer project: produce intake-ready playbooks and refer NYC Catholic schools, childcare centers and youth nonprofits to the NYU clinic, rather than competing with it.
- Philanthropic funders (Google.org, Craig Newmark, Spencer) are already paying for this public good. Funders could also attach security conditions to grants for youth-serving nonprofits. That is a lever the project could propose to foundations serving Catholic education.
- The UAlbany clinic is insurance-industry funded (Spencer Foundation). This suggests insurers may fund clinics as risk-reduction, a possible pitch to Catholic Mutual, Church Mutual or Brotherhood.

### Gaps
- No published outcome data (posture change, incidents avoided) from any clinic was found.
- No Catholic-school-specific or diocesan cyber clinic was found. I did not search NCEA or Catholic university programs specifically.
- I found nothing on Columbia or CUNY clinics.
- The Consortium's own annual-report figures were not fetched directly; the 61 clinics and 700 organizations figures come via secondary reporting.

## Cross-cutting assessment of the hypothesis (synthesis for the report writer)
- **Supported:** MFA is free in Microsoft Business Basic and Google Workspace, and CISA scanning is free. Yet email fraud remains about 60% of small-business claims, and only about 1,500 schools had enrolled in CISA scanning (older figure). The constraint is attention and execution, not tools.
- **Partly supported:** money matters at the margin. The Microsoft Business Premium grant ending (Jul 2025) and MS-ISAC fees (Oct 2025) removed free security layers, and small organizations rarely fill new budget lines.
- **Levers ranked by inferred strength:** diocesan or association master-policy conditions and procurement terms; insurer renewal questionnaires; funder grant conditions; free services with done-for-you enrollment; clinics (high quality, low capacity); guides (necessary but not sufficient).
- **Key unknown:** whether Catholic Mutual imposes any technical conditions. A direct inquiry to a diocesan risk manager would settle it.
