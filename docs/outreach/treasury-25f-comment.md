**To:** Internal Revenue Service, Department of the Treasury
**Re:** Federal Scholarship Tax Credit, proposed regulations (REG-117199-25)
**Subject:** Protecting families' income information held by scholarship granting organizations

I volunteer on cybersecurity for schools and small nonprofits, focusing on
organizations that hold children's information without security staff. I
write about one practical matter: the household income information that
scholarship granting organizations (SGOs) will collect to verify
eligibility.

The preamble states a principle I strongly support: the categorical
eligibility method and the safe harbors are meant to avoid "requiring SGOs
to collect more sensitive household-income information than is necessary."
The suggestions below are meant to carry that principle through the final
rule. As proposed, the rule says nothing about how SGOs protect, retain or
dispose of this information. Under the direct income verification method,
that information includes tax returns, IRS transcripts, Forms W-2 and pay
stubs.

**Why this matters now.** Many SGOs will be new, small nonprofits with no
security staff, starting operations in 2027 across 30 or more states. Recent
incidents in state school-choice programs show the risks are real. In 2025,
phishing aimed at South Carolina's scholarship program stole education
providers' payment-platform credentials. In 2026, Missouri's program
records, including student names, parent contact details, award amounts and
schools, were exposed on a public state website for nearly a year. A family's
tax return in an SGO's files is a valuable target for identity theft, and
copies held by an SGO are not covered by the confidentiality protections of
section 6103.

## Recommendations

**1. Expand categorical eligibility (responding to the request for comment
in the preamble).** Consider adding:
- Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), to the
  extent their income limits fall below the section 25F cap (CHIP limits
  vary by state);
- individually determined eligibility for free or reduced-price school
  meals, through direct certification or an approved individual
  application. This would be distinct from school-wide eligibility, which
  the preamble rightly excludes;
- State and Tribal programs whose income limits are at or below the
  section 25F cap.

Each relies on an eligibility determination a government agency has
already made. Each lets an SGO verify eligibility from an award letter
instead of a tax return.

**2. State a preference for the least sensitive method.** The final rule or
preamble should say that SGOs are encouraged to use the categorical method
or a safe harbor where available. They should use direct income
verification only when needed, collecting only the documents necessary to
make the determination.

**3. Allow SGOs to keep the determination, not the documents.** Clarify in
§1.25F-4 that an SGO may satisfy its recordkeeping and audit obligations by
retaining a verification record: the method used, the type of document
reviewed, the date, the reviewer and the result. It should be free to
securely destroy copies of tax returns and similar documents once
verification is complete. Where copies must be kept, they should be kept no
longer than the applicable audit or limitations period. This reduces the
amount of sensitive data exposed in a breach without weakening audits,
which can test the verification records.

**4. Define "other relevant data sources."** The direct method allows
verification "through other relevant data sources." If this includes
consumer reporting agencies or commercial data, the final rule should
require:
- notice to the family that such a source was used;
- a way for the family to dispute an inaccurate result;
- a prohibition on using data obtained for eligibility for any other
  purpose, such as fundraising, marketing or sale.

**5. Clarify that states may require reasonable data protection.**
Proposed §1.25F-5(e)(2) bars states from requiring SGOs to operate "in a
manner that is more restrictive than" the federal requirements. The final
rule should make clear that this does not prevent a state from requiring
reasonable data security, privacy and breach-notification practices of
SGOs. Such requirements do not limit which students or schools may
participate. They fit within the state's existing role under
§1.25F-5(e)(1)(ii) to require documentation reasonably tailored to
preventing fraud and abuse. Without this clarification, states may hesitate
to require basic protections.

**6. Include a few basic safeguards in the annual programmatic audit.**
Proposed §1.25F-4(e) already requires an annual financial and programmatic
audit. Its scope could include whether an SGO that holds applicant income
documents:
- requires multi-factor authentication for accounts that can access them;
- stores them encrypted;
- limits access to staff who need it;
- has a written plan for notifying affected families and the state after a
  breach.

These controls are inexpensive and available in mainstream office
software. A short, concrete list also suits the smallest SGOs, which under
§1.25F-4(e)(2)(ii) may be audited by a committee of independent volunteers
rather than security professionals. Audits should test these controls
rather than accept a checkbox. An
analysis of New York City's student-data vendor disclosures found that
95.7% of 972 entries chose an identical, pre-written encryption statement.
They included a vendor that regulators later alleged stored student data
unencrypted. (Analysis: https://github.com/davelee0/security/tree/main/research/nyc-2d)

**7. Help taxpayers recognize legitimate SGOs.** A credit that returns up to
$1,700 per taxpayer will be used as a lure by scammers impersonating SGOs.
Including each SGO's official website address on the IRS SGO list
(§1.25F-5(d)(9)) and on State SGO lists (§1.25F-5(d)) would let taxpayers and email
providers distinguish real SGOs from impostors. A consumer alert before the
2027 filing season would also help.

**8. In future guidance, consider a yes-or-no income confirmation.** A
consent-based IRS service that tells an SGO only whether a household is
under the income limit, similar in spirit to the consent-based IRS data
exchange now used for federal student aid, would let SGOs verify
eligibility without ever holding a family's tax return.

Thank you for considering these comments. A program that serves families
well should also keep their information safe.
