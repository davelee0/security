**To:** Internal Revenue Service, Department of the Treasury  
**Re:** Federal Scholarship Tax Credit, proposed regulations (REG-117199-25; 91 FR 62818, October 2, 2026)  
**Docket:** IRS-2026-1354  
**Submitted by:** Dave Lee, New York, NY

I submit this comment as a private individual and community volunteer. It addresses one part of the proposed regulations: the household income information that scholarship granting organizations (SGOs) will collect under proposed §1.25F-3(c)(6), and how that information is protected once collected.

The preamble explains that the categorical eligibility method and the safe harbors are intended to avoid "requiring SGOs to collect more sensitive household-income information than is necessary." I support that principle. The recommendations below would carry it through the final regulations. As proposed, the regulations do not address how SGOs safeguard, retain or dispose of the tax returns, IRS transcripts, Forms W-2 and pay stubs collected under the direct income verification method.

## Summary of recommendations

1. Add Medicaid, CHIP and individually determined school-meal eligibility to the categorical eligibility method (§1.25F-3(c)(6)(iii)(B)), in response to the preamble's request for comments.
2. Encourage SGOs to use the categorical method or a safe harbor where available, and to collect only the documents a determination requires.
3. Clarify that SGOs may keep a record of each eligibility determination instead of copies of the underlying documents (§1.25F-4).
4. Define "other relevant data sources," and attach notice, dispute and purpose-limitation conditions to their use.
5. Clarify that §1.25F-5(e)(2) does not bar states from requiring reasonable data security, privacy and breach-notification practices.
6. Add a short list of testable safeguards to the annual programmatic audit (§1.25F-4(e)(3)).
7. Include each SGO's official website address on the IRS SGO list and State SGO lists (§1.25F-5(d)).
8. In future guidance, consider a consent-based yes-or-no income confirmation from the IRS.

## Why this matters

Under the direct method, an SGO may hold a family's tax return or IRS transcript. These documents show a household's income, dependents and address, and a tax return shows Social Security numbers. Once collected, they are a lasting target for identity theft.

Many SGOs will be new or small organizations. The smallest may meet the audit requirement through a committee of independent persons (§1.25F-4(e)(2)(ii)) and are unlikely to employ security staff.

State school-choice programs show that the risks are practical. In 2025, a phishing campaign aimed at providers in South Carolina's Education Scholarship Trust Fund sought their payment-platform credentials and banking information. In 2026, spreadsheets posted on the Missouri State Treasurer's website were reported to have exposed three years of MOScholars records, including student names, parent contact information, award amounts and schools, for nearly a year.

Copies of tax returns held by an SGO do not appear to be protected by section 6103, which governs disclosure by the IRS and other officials. Many SGOs will also fall outside the federal and state privacy laws that apply to schools. The final regulations are therefore an important opportunity to set expectations for how these records are handled.

## Recommendations

### 1. Expand the categorical eligibility method

The preamble requests comments on other needs-based programs that should be included in the categorical eligibility verification method. I suggest adding:

- Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), where the program's income limit in the state does not exceed the section 25F limit;
- eligibility for free or reduced-price school meals determined for the individual household, through direct certification or an approved household application. This is distinct from school-wide eligibility, which the preamble appropriately excludes;
- State and Tribal assistance programs with household income limits at or below the section 25F limit.

Each relies on a determination a government agency has already made, and each lets an SGO verify eligibility from an award letter rather than a tax return. School meal programs already rely on this approach through direct certification.

### 2. Prefer the least sensitive method

The final regulations or preamble should state that SGOs are encouraged to use the categorical method or a safe harbor where one is available, and to use the direct method only when necessary, collecting only the documents needed to make the determination. This follows the preamble's stated intent and reduces the sensitive information SGOs hold without reducing the accuracy of determinations.

### 3. Keep the determination, not the documents

The final regulations should clarify in §1.25F-4 that an SGO meets its recordkeeping obligations for household income verification by retaining a record of each determination, and may securely dispose of copies of the underlying documents once the determination is made. Illustrative language:

> For each household income determination under §1.25F-3(c)(6), an SGO must retain a record of the verification method used, the type of documentation reviewed, the date of the review, the person who performed it, and the result. An SGO is not required to retain copies of tax returns, IRS transcripts, Forms W-2, pay stubs or similar documents after the determination. Any copies retained should be kept no longer than needed for the audit required by §1.25F-4(e) or the applicable period of limitations.

The annual programmatic audit can test these verification records. Copies kept after the decision add exposure to identity theft without improving administration.

### 4. Define "other relevant data sources"

The direct method allows income to be "verified through other relevant data sources." If this includes consumer reporting agencies or commercial data providers, the final regulations should require that:

- the household is told which source was used;
- the household can dispute an inaccurate result and submit documentation instead;
- information obtained to determine eligibility is not used for any other purpose, including fundraising, marketing or sale.

Commercial data can be inaccurate, and a family denied a scholarship on that basis needs a remedy. The purpose limitation should apply to all eligibility information, however it was obtained.

### 5. Confirm that states may require data protection

Proposed §1.25F-5(e)(2) provides that a state "may not require SGOs to operate in a manner that is more restrictive than" the requirements of section 25F(c)(5) and §1.25F-3(b) and (c). The final regulations should state that reasonable requirements for data security, privacy and breach notification are not prohibited by this paragraph.

Such requirements do not limit which students or schools may participate. They fit within the states' role under §1.25F-5(e)(1)(ii) to require documentation reasonably tailored to preventing fraud and abuse. Without this clarification, states may hesitate to require basic protections.

### 6. Add testable safeguards to the programmatic audit

Proposed §1.25F-4(e)(3) sets the minimum content of the annual audit. For an SGO that holds applicant income documents, the audit could also confirm that the SGO:

- requires multi-factor authentication for accounts that can access those documents;
- stores them encrypted;
- limits access to staff who need it;
- has a written breach-response plan that includes notifying affected families and the state.

These controls are inexpensive, are built into mainstream office software, and can be checked by a volunteer audit committee as well as by a professional auditor. The audit should confirm them with evidence rather than accept a statement that they are in place. An analysis of the 972 student-data vendor disclosures published by New York City Public Schools found that 95.7% used an identical, pre-written encryption statement. They included the disclosure of a vendor that the Federal Trade Commission later alleged had stored student data unencrypted.

### 7. Publish SGO website addresses

A credit of up to $1,700 per taxpayer is likely to attract scams that impersonate SGOs. Including each SGO's official website address on the IRS SGO list (§1.25F-5(d)(9)) and on State SGO lists (§1.25F-5(d)) would let taxpayers confirm they are dealing with a listed SGO. A consumer alert before the 2027 filing season would also help.

### 8. Consider a yes-or-no confirmation in future guidance

A consent-based IRS service that tells an SGO only whether a household is under the income limit would let SGOs verify eligibility without ever holding a family's tax return. The consent-based exchange of IRS data now used for federal student aid shows that this kind of arrangement can work.

Thank you for considering these comments.

Respectfully submitted,
